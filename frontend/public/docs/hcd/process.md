## Overview

The HCD process is how a team moves from a problem to a solution that works for the people it serves. It is a loop, not a line: each pass through it produces evidence that shapes the next. This page describes the phases, what each one produces, and how to fit them into agency delivery.

The phases follow the activities in ISO 9241-210 and the Design Council's Double Diamond (discover, define, develop, deliver). Use the names your program already uses; what matters is that each activity happens.

## The phases

### 1. Discover: understand the problem and the context

Learn how things work today, before deciding what to change.

- **Questions:** Who is involved? What are they trying to do? What gets in the way? What rules, systems and offices shape the experience?
- **Activities:** stakeholder interviews, review of existing research, analytics and support data, contextual inquiry and interviews with users (see [Research methods](/hcd/research-methods)).
- **Outputs:** research findings, a first [journey map](/research/journey-maps) of the current experience, a list of assumptions to test.

### 2. Define: agree on the problem worth solving

Turn what you learned into a clear statement of the problem and how you will know it is solved.

- **Activities:** synthesis workshops, affinity mapping, prioritizing [insights](/research/insights), writing problem statements and success measures.
- **Outputs:** problem statement, user needs, success measures (for example task completion rate, time to complete, error rate, satisfaction), scope for the first release.

A good problem statement names the user, their need and the evidence: *"People renewing a benefit online abandon the form at the income step because it asks for figures they don't have to hand (12 of 15 participants)."*

### 3. Develop: explore and test solutions

Generate several options, make them testable quickly, and let users show you which work.

- **Activities:** sketching, co-design sessions, prototyping (paper, clickable, coded), usability testing, content testing, accessibility review.
- **Outputs:** tested design direction, prototypes in the [interactives library](/design-assets/interactives), usability findings and the changes made because of them.
- Build with the design system's [components](/components) and [patterns](/patterns) so that prototypes look and behave like the final product.

### 4. Deliver: build, launch and learn

Ship in small increments and keep measuring.

- **Activities:** design and engineering pairing, accessibility testing with assistive technology, usability testing on the built product, phased rollout, monitoring analytics and feedback.
- **Outputs:** the released service, a measurement plan, an [HCD report](/research/hcd-reports) that records what was learned.

After launch the loop starts again: what people do with the live service is the best evidence for the next change.

## Fitting HCD into agency delivery

### Agile teams

Run research a sprint ahead of development. While engineers build what was tested last sprint, researchers and designers test what will be built next. Keep a standing slot each week or sprint for sessions with users so research never waits on scheduling.

### Acquisitions

HCD can and should be written into contracts. Ask vendors to describe how they will involve users, include research and usability testing as deliverables, and evaluate proposals partly on demonstrated HCD practice. See [Improving UX through acquisition](/getting-started/improving-ux-through-acquisition) for more.

### Governance and reviews

Bring evidence to reviews. A decision supported by research findings is easier to defend than one supported by preference. The [HCD council](/hcd/council) can review research plans and findings for larger efforts.

### Paperwork Reduction Act

Some research methods count as an information collection under the Paperwork Reduction Act. Plan for this in the discover phase, not the week before testing. See [Recruiting users](/hcd/recruiting-users).

## Roles

| Role | Contribution |
| --- | --- |
| Product owner | Owns the problem and the outcome; makes trade-offs with evidence |
| User researcher | Plans and runs research; makes sure findings reach decisions |
| Designer (interaction, visual, service) | Explores and prototypes solutions; designs the end-to-end experience |
| Content designer | Makes sure people understand what to do, in plain language |
| Engineer | Shapes what's feasible; builds accessible, tested code |
| Accessibility specialist | Sets the standard, reviews designs and tests with assistive technology |
| Program and policy staff | Explain the rules and what can change; carry findings back to policy |

Everyone on the team should observe research sessions. Two hours of watching users each six weeks is a widely used minimum.

## Checklist

- [ ] We have spoken with users before deciding what to build.
- [ ] We have a written problem statement and success measures.
- [ ] We tested more than one option.
- [ ] People with disabilities took part in research and testing.
- [ ] We know whether our research needs PRA clearance and have planned for it.
- [ ] Findings and decisions are recorded where future teams can find them.
- [ ] We are measuring the live service against our success measures.
