## Why we do research

User research replaces assumptions about people with evidence. It tells a team what problem is worth solving, whether a design works, and whether a live service is meeting people's needs. Done well, it saves money: changing a sketch costs far less than changing a released system.

Research is a team activity. Researchers plan and lead it, but everyone who makes decisions about the product should observe sessions and take part in analysis.

## Two kinds of research

| | Generative (discovery) | Evaluative |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Question** | What do people need? What is the problem? | Does this design work? |
| **When** | Before and early in design | Throughout design and after launch |
| **Typical methods** | Interviews, contextual inquiry, diary studies, service safaris | Usability testing, tree testing, first-click tests, accessibility testing, analytics |
| **Output** | Insights, user needs, journey maps | Usability issues, measures, recommendations |

Both are needed. Teams that only evaluate polish the wrong thing; teams that only explore never confirm their solution works.

## Planning a study

Write a short research plan before you recruit anyone. It keeps the study focused and makes approvals faster. A plan answers:

1. **Background:** what decision is this research informing?
2. **Research questions:** the three to five things you need to learn. These are not the questions you ask participants.
3. **Method:** how you will answer them, and why that method fits (see [Research methods](/hcd/research-methods)).
4. **Participants:** who, how many, and how you will reach them (see [Recruiting users](/hcd/recruiting-users)).
5. **Logistics:** dates, location or tools, roles (facilitator, note-taker, observers).
6. **Consent and data:** what you will record, how you will store it, who can see it and when it will be deleted.
7. **Compliance:** whether the Paperwork Reduction Act applies, and any privacy review your agency requires.

## Running research ethically

People who take part in our research are giving us their time and trust. Protect both.

- **Informed consent.** Explain the purpose, what will be recorded, how it will be used and that taking part is voluntary and does not affect any benefit, case or job. Get consent before recording.
- **No effect on services.** Make clear that what participants say will not change their eligibility, case or standing with the agency, and make sure that is true.
- **Collect the minimum.** Don't collect personal information you don't need. Avoid asking for case numbers, Social Security numbers or financial details.
- **Protect the data.** Store recordings and notes in approved agency systems with access limited to the research team. Keep identifiable data separate from findings. Delete it on the schedule you promised.
- **De-identify findings.** Use participant numbers, not names, in notes and reports. Watch for details that identify someone indirectly (a rare job title, a small office).
- **Privacy Act.** If you keep records about people that you retrieve by their name or another identifier, your agency may need a System of Records Notice. Talk to your privacy office early.
- **Accessibility.** Make sure people with disabilities can take part: accessible consent forms and tools, captioning, interpreters and extra time when needed.

## Making research count

Research only helps if it reaches decisions.

- Hold a debrief within a day of sessions, while memory is fresh.
- Synthesize as a team: group observations into patterns, and patterns into [insights](/research/insights).
- Write findings people can act on, with the evidence behind each one (see [Research reports](/hcd/research-reports)).
- Store studies, reports and insights in the research libraries so other teams can build on them:
  - [User studies](/research/user-studies)
  - [HCD reports](/research/hcd-reports)
  - [Research insights](/research/insights)
  - [Journey maps](/research/journey-maps)
- Track what changed because of the research.
