## Recruit the right people

Who you talk to determines what you learn. Recruiting the wrong participants, or only the easiest ones to reach, produces confident findings that don't hold up when the service launches.

- **Recruit by behavior and need, not job title or demographics alone.** "People who have renewed a benefit online in the last year" is more useful than "adults aged 25 to 45."
- **Include the edges.** People with disabilities, people with limited English proficiency, older adults, people with low digital literacy, people in rural areas and people using mobile devices or slow connections are often the ones a service fails first.
- **Include staff.** Caseworkers, call-center agents and field staff know where the service breaks and use internal tools all day.
- **Avoid convenience samples.** Colleagues, friends and the same small group of frequent volunteers are not your users.

## Plan for the Paperwork Reduction Act

The Paperwork Reduction Act (PRA) requires OMB approval before an agency collects information from ten or more members of the public using identical questions. Some research methods fall under it; others generally don't. Settle this early with your agency's information collection clearance officer: it can add months to a schedule if left late.

As a general guide (confirm with your clearance officer):

| Usually needs clearance | Usually doesn't |
| --- | --- |
| Surveys and questionnaires sent to ten or more members of the public | Research with federal employees acting in their official role |
| Standardized questions asked of ten or more people | Usability testing that observes people doing tasks, with non-standardized follow-up questions |
| Screeners with identical questions, when used with ten or more people | Research with fewer than ten members of the public |

Many agencies hold a **generic clearance** for customer research, which allows individual studies to be approved quickly under an umbrella approval. Ask whether yours does before planning a study around a full clearance.

## Where to find participants

- **Agency channels:** opt-in lists from email newsletters, a "help us improve this service" link on the website, or a question at the end of a transaction.
- **Front-line staff and partners:** service centers, community organizations, advocacy groups and other agencies that already work with the people you need.
- **Organizations for people with disabilities:** local and national disability organizations can help reach people who use assistive technology.
- **Recruiting vendors and panels:** useful for hard-to-find profiles; check your contract vehicle and confirm the vendor meets your agency's privacy and security requirements.
- **Internal staff directories and communities of practice** for research with employees.

Never recruit through channels that could make people think taking part affects their case, benefit or job.

## Screening

A short screener confirms each person fits the study. Keep it brief and neutral:

- Ask about behavior ("How did you last renew your registration?") rather than asking people to classify themselves.
- Hide the "right" answer so people can't guess what qualifies them.
- Ask about access needs and the devices and assistive technology people use, so you can prepare.
- Collect only what you need to schedule and select participants, and delete screener data for people you don't select.

## Incentives

Incentives show respect for people's time and improve turnout, but rules vary across agencies. Check with your legal or ethics office before offering one. Federal employees taking part as part of their job generally cannot accept incentives. If you can't offer an incentive, be clear about why the research matters and keep sessions short.

## Scheduling and consent

- Offer sessions at different times, including outside working hours, and in the format people can manage (remote, phone, in person).
- Send a confirmation that says what will happen, how long it takes, what will be recorded and who to contact.
- Send the consent form in advance in an accessible format, and in the participant's language when needed.
- Ask what accommodations people need: interpreters (including sign language), captioning, a particular screen reader, extra time, breaks.
- Recruit a few more people than you need; expect some no-shows.

## Recruiting checklist

- [ ] Participant criteria describe behaviors and needs.
- [ ] The sample includes people with disabilities and other people the service is likely to fail.
- [ ] PRA question settled with the clearance officer.
- [ ] Privacy review complete; data storage and deletion planned.
- [ ] Screener tested and neutral.
- [ ] Incentive approved, or a clear decision not to offer one.
- [ ] Consent form and session materials accessible and in plain language.
