## What interviews are good for

Interviews help you understand people's goals, experiences, motivations and the context they work in. They are good at uncovering problems you didn't know about and at explaining the *why* behind behavior seen elsewhere.

They are poor at predicting what people will do, measuring how common something is, or judging whether a design is usable. For those, use [usability testing](/hcd/research-methods), analytics or surveys.

## Before the interview

### Write a discussion guide

A discussion guide lists the topics and questions you plan to cover, in a rough order. It keeps sessions consistent across interviewers without turning them into a script.

A typical 45 to 60 minute guide:

1. **Introduction (5 minutes):** who you are, the purpose, how the session works, consent and recording.
2. **Warm-up (5 minutes):** easy questions about the person's role or situation.
3. **Main topics (30 to 40 minutes):** open questions about their experience, organized by theme.
4. **Wrap-up (5 minutes):** anything else they want to say, questions for you, thanks and next steps.

### Prepare the session

- Test the guide and any tools in a pilot session with a colleague.
- Check the technology: recording, screen sharing, captions and compatibility with the participant's assistive technology.
- Agree on roles: one facilitator, one note-taker, and observers who stay quiet (or send questions to the facilitator in a back channel).
- Re-read the screener answers so you know who you are talking to.

## During the interview

### Open well

- Thank the person and explain that you are testing ideas and services, not them; there are no wrong answers.
- Explain that taking part has no effect on any benefit, case or job.
- Confirm consent to record and remind them they can stop or skip a question at any time.

### Ask good questions

| Instead of | Ask |
| --- | --- |
| "Would you use a feature that…?" (prediction) | "Tell me about the last time you…" (past behavior) |
| "Was it easy to find?" (leading, yes/no) | "How did you find it?" |
| "Do you like the new design?" (opinion of our work) | "Walk me through how you would do this." |
| "Why didn't you use the online form?" (sounds like blame) | "What made you choose to call instead?" |
| Two questions at once | One question, then wait |

Useful follow-ups: "Tell me more about that." "What happened next?" "Can you show me?" "What did you expect to happen?" "How did that make you feel?"

### Listen more than you talk

- Let silences run; people often add the most useful detail after a pause.
- Repeat back what you heard in their words to check understanding.
- Don't explain or defend the design, and don't correct the participant.
- Watch for differences between what people say they do and what they show you.
- Follow interesting threads even if they aren't in the guide; return to the guide when the thread runs out.

### Take notes that help analysis

- Record observations and quotes, separately from your interpretation.
- Note timestamps for important moments so you can find them in the recording.
- Use participant numbers, never names.

## Interviewing people with disabilities

- Ask in advance what the person needs and prepare for it: a sign language interpreter, live captions, materials in advance, a particular platform or format.
- Let the person use their own devices and assistive technology where possible.
- Allow extra time and breaks without making a point of it.
- Talk to the participant, not to their interpreter or support person.
- Ask about their experience directly; don't make assumptions about what their disability means for them.

## Interviewing staff

Staff can describe policy constraints, workarounds and common problems from the public. Make clear that the interview is not an evaluation of their performance, keep what they say confidential from their management, and report themes rather than attributing comments to individuals.

## After the interview

- Hold a 15-minute debrief with everyone who observed, the same day.
- Write up notes while they are fresh; tidy the transcript if you have one.
- Store recordings and notes in the approved location with access limited to the team, and delete them on schedule.
- Start synthesis once you have a few sessions: group observations into themes and look for patterns across participants. See [Research reports](/hcd/research-reports).
